<li><span class="num">1.</span><strong>Endogenous Markups in Demand and Production Networks</strong> <a href="/papers/network_markup/">[PDF]</a>
<span class="co">Single-authored</span>
<span class="pres"><em>Presentations</em>: Network Science &amp; Economics 2026 Luxembourg, EWMES 2026 Warsaw†</span>
<details class="abs"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>How costly is market power when firms are connected as substitutes, complements and suppliers? I answer this question in a model of endogenous markups with all three connections, calibrated to U.S. public firms in 2022. To measure complementarity, a large language model reads the firms’ 10-K filings and judges, for every pair, whether one user needs products of both for the same task. In this economy, market power costs 19 percent of total surplus at the efficient allocation. Supply chains amplify this cost, since markups compounding along them multiply the loss from firms’ own price rises by 2.5. Substitution lowers the cost and complementarity raises it, because a markup on one good also raises the demand for its substitutes and cuts the demand for its complements. Splitting the cost into combinations of price changes, I show that the most costly combinations need complements. These combinations also govern how far output subsidies can undo the cost. Along each combination, firms pass on only part of a subsidy, and the least in the most costly ones, so a subsidy as large as the markups does not restore marginal-cost pricing. In the calibrated economy, a dollar of uniform subsidy buys 1.08 dollars of welfare, against 3.41 at fixed markups. For the same reason, when AI raises the productivity of the firms that buy it, the welfare gain is 45 percent of what it would be at fixed markups.</p></details></li>
<li><span class="num">2.</span><strong>Public Allocation and Private Supply: A Housing Assignment Approach</strong> <a href="/papers/housing_spillover/">[PDF]</a>
<span class="co">with Jimmy Ho (WUSTL), Yulin Hong (CityUHK) and <a href="https://mbwong.com/">Michael B. Wong</a> (HKU)</span>
<span class="pres"><em>Presentations</em>: EMUEA 2025 Berlin, HKU*, Jinan-SMU Conference on Urban and Regional Economics 2025*, ASSA Annual Meeting 2026 Philadelphia*, EEA-ESEM 2026 Dublin, UPF Internal Applied Lunch</span>
<details class="abs"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>How does public housing allocation affect private rents and supply across quality segments? Motivated by patterns in Hong Kong’s rental market, we develop a tractable two-quality housing assignment model with rationed public housing, endogenous construction, and costly conversion of existing dwellings. With imperfect targeting, more generous public housing shifts private demand toward low quality. When new construction is high quality and conversion is sufficiently costly, low-quality private rents rise while high-quality rents fall. In a calibration to Hong Kong, raising public rents or converting public flats to private rental benefits low-quality private renters, but subsidy losses outweigh these gains: expected renter utility falls in every income group when fiscal proceeds are not redistributed.</p></details></li>
